On the 2026–27 academic job market

Ph.D. expected May 2027

I work in political economy, public choice, and economic sociology. My research asks how people build and sustain peaceful cooperation without central direction, and how crises shape the scope of government.

My job market paper, written with Christopher Coyne and published in the Journal of Public Finance and Public Choice, traces the Defense Production Act of 1950 from its roots in earlier crises through its expansion in later ones. My dissertation develops a bottom-up economic theory of peace, following how people’s pursuit of relational goods shapes their conduct and expectations, first within the family and then outward to communities, strangers, and trading partners.

I teach principles and upper-level courses, in person and online. I am a Ph.D. Fellow with the F. A. Hayek Program for Advanced Study in Philosophy, Politics, and Economics at the Mercatus Center and an Adam Smith Program Fellow in George Mason’s Department of Economics.

Research

Job market paper

The Defense Production Act: An Enduring Legacy of Crises

with Christopher J. Coyne

Journal of Public Finance and Public Choice, vol. 41, no. 1 (2026), pp. 2–26

The Defense Production Act of 1950 gives the president discretionary authority over domestic economic activity in the name of national defense. We examine the Act’s evolution through the lens of the ratchet effect, which explains how crises can lead to lasting growth in government. Tracing the Act’s origins through World War I, the Great Depression, World War II, and the Korean War, we show how responses to past crises set precedents that lay the groundwork for later expansions of government in ways few anticipated.

Working papers

  • Peace as a Social Process and Choice

    I develop a bottom-up economic theory of peace. As people pursue relational goods, they adjust their conduct to one another and form expectations about people they have never met, first within the family, then among friends and groups, and finally among strangers and trading partners. I argue that this process makes violence costly enough to keep it off the table, producing peace without relying on state capacity.

  • In Defense of Peace: Rethinking the Quintessential Public Good

    I revisit the standard treatment of national defense as a public good in light of the security dilemma.

Teaching

I believe education is a co-produced good. Students learn most when a course gives them reasons to engage all semester, not only before exams, so I build those reasons into the design of every class.

In person, I give frequent short quizzes, ask students to take notes by hand, and assign Robert Frank’s economic naturalist essay, in which students use economics to explain something puzzling they noticed in everyday life. Online, I replace quizzes with open-ended reflection essays on the readings and answer each one with detailed feedback.
